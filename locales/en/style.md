# Style: English

> **Status: first pass.** Written against the shared taxonomy, not translated
> from the French pack. It has not been signed by a second native reviewer.
> `native_reviewed` is `false` in `lint.yml` and every skill using this pack
> says so out loud.

Exact strings live in `lint.yml`. This file says why a category matters and
what the repair looks like.

## Hard rules

1. **Open with the answer.** Not with context, not with a restatement of the
   question.
2. **One idea per paragraph**, three or four sentences at most.
3. **Take a position.** If one option is better, say so.
4. **End on the last useful idea.** No summary, no moral.

## The ten categories

### grandiose-verbs

The verb inflates an ordinary action into an event. The reader hears a
brochure.

> This approach will transform your pipeline.

> We went from $0.10 to $0.007 per contact.

`leverage` deserves a line of its own. As a noun in finance it is precise. As a
verb replacing "use", it is the single most common tell in B2B English.

### hollow-jargon

Nouns that sound like expertise and carry no claim. Test: remove the word. If
the sentence says the same thing, it was filler.

### filler-crutches

Phrases that buy time before the sentence starts. "In today's fast-paced world"
is the canonical case: it is true of every year since 1750 and therefore says
nothing.

### fake-hooks

The opener announces the subject instead of stating it. On LinkedIn this is the
worst possible place for it, because those lines are the only ones most people
will read.

> Let's dive into the world of data enrichment.

> 507 of the 700 leads I pulled had no usable email.

### schoolbook-transitions

Graded-essay connectives. Nobody says "furthermore" out loud. The repair is
almost always to cut the connective and put nothing in its place.

### summarizing-closers

The ending repeats the post instead of ending it. If the last idea is good it
stands alone. If it is not, a summary will not save it.

### forced-empathy

Validation addressed to nobody. "Great question" inside a post answers an
interlocutor who does not exist.

### negative-parallelism

**The dominant English tell of 2026.** "It's not X, it's Y." "This isn't just
X. It's Y." The shape is now a signature, and it gives the impression of one
idea while making two halves of two.

**No mechanical repair.** Substituting the shape produces another sentence with
the same problem. The repair is two separate statements, and only the author
knows which two.

> It's not a volume problem, it's a targeting problem.

> Volume changed nothing. We doubled the list and the calendar stayed empty.
> What moved it was the company-size filter.

Weight it above the French equivalent. In English it is the first thing a
reader who is tired of AI text will notice.

### dramatic-fragmentation

One-word lines. "Let that sink in." "Read that again." "Every. Single. Time."
The device asks the reader to be impressed instead of being impressive.

Also here: "The result?" alone on a line, and the trailing single-word
paragraph used as a drum beat.

### typography

- **No em dash.** This one has a real cost, and it should be stated plainly:
  the em dash is legitimate English punctuation and this rule gives it up. It
  is given up because it has become the number one machine signature, and a
  comma, a colon or a full stop covers every case at a small stylistic loss.
  A pack for another language should not copy this rule without asking whether
  the cost is the same there.
- **No space before `: ; ! ?`.** English does not take one. A French writer
  publishing in English gets this wrong constantly.
- **Straight or curly quotes**, consistently, either is fine.
- **No emoji**, unless the profile explicitly turns them on.

## Form of address

English does not force the formal or informal choice, which removes a decision
and hides a trap: the register still exists, it just moves into contractions
and verb choice. "Do not" versus "don't" carries as much as "vous" versus "tu".

Read it off the corpus. Do not set it.
